A few people asked me recently how I prepare for interviews, especially the more senior ones where people are trying to work out how you think and what you might be capable of. There were also questions about whether I remain my authentic self, what people look for in public service leaders, and whether preparing too much means putting on a different image.

I don't actually interview very often. When one matters, though, I prepare quite a lot in the days before it: speaking answers aloud, recording myself, standing in front of a mirror, rehearsing my opening and mentally running through the whole session.

But the more I thought about this, the more I realised that most interview preparation doesn't happen in those few days.

It happened in the years before.

## Most of the preparation already happened

You cannot prepare yourself into being a good candidate a week before an interview.

You can improve your delivery and think through likely questions. You can make sure you don't ramble for five minutes before finally reaching the point. What you cannot manufacture very easily is several years of actually knowing your work, getting things done and learning enough to make useful connections when somebody takes you somewhere unfamiliar.

That is probably how I would think about interviews now.

First, know your stuff.

Then get stuff done.

Then show what you can do with what you know, including when the problem in front of you isn't something you have directly done before.

The interview preparation afterwards is about making sure people can actually see all of that in the fairly short time you have with them.

## A disclaimer before I pretend to know everything

I don't know the secret scoring rubric for every public service interview, and I am definitely not saying that what follows is some official formula.

A lot of this came from advice people gave me over the years. Some I learnt from being interviewed myself, and later I started noticing the same things from sitting on the other side and interviewing people.

There is inevitably some of my own bias in here too. These are things I personally find convincing when somebody is sitting across from me, so treat this as accumulated experience rather than the Public Service Interview Bible.

## First, know your stuff

This has to come first.

If you tell me you worked on something, I expect you to understand it properly. I don't mean knowing every obscure fact or being able to recite the entire project history; I mean understanding what you actually did, why decisions were made and what happened when things became difficult.

You can normally tell when somebody really lived with a problem because there is another layer underneath the first answer. Ask why and they can go deeper. Challenge one assumption and they know where it came from, or at least know where their own knowledge ends.

It is equally obvious when somebody has memorised the first layer.

You can practise enough interview answers to sound knowledgeable for a while, but somebody who understands the subject can usually keep digging. Eventually we find out whether you know your stuff.

### Knowing stuff isn't knowing trivia

I would be careful here because there is a difference between depth and testing whether somebody happens to know the same things I know.

Someone coming from another area may use completely different terminology and still understand the underlying problem very well. Someone who has worked in exactly the same domain for ten years might have surprisingly little depth once you move beyond the familiar answer.

So I am less interested in trivia than whether there is genuine understanding behind the experience someone claims.

Sometimes the best follow-up question is simply:

> Why?

Then ask why again.

## Then, get stuff done

Knowing things is great. I also want to know whether anything actually happened because you were there.

What did you deliver? What changed? Did you take something from an idea to something people actually used? Did you move something that had been stuck for months, or persuade people who initially thought your proposal was rubbish?

The outcome does not always need to be a giant success story. Sometimes the right thing was to stop a project. Sometimes circumstances made the original outcome impossible and you had to change direction.

What I care about is what _you_ actually did.

There is a difference between being around good work and being responsible for making some of it happen.

That difference usually becomes fairly obvious once people start asking questions.

## Then show what you can do with what you know

This is probably where interviews become much more interesting to me.

We live in an increasingly complicated world and nobody can know everything. Eventually every one of us reaches a problem we have never encountered before, so I care quite a lot about what somebody does once they reach the edge of what they already know.

I like people who can connect dots.

Someone sees a technology problem and remembers something they learnt from operations. A policy problem starts looking like an incentive problem they saw somewhere else. They borrow an approach from another industry, adapt it, and suddenly there is a solution that looked innovative only because nobody else thought to bring those two things together.

The best people I have worked with seem to do this quite naturally. They recognise patterns and applications across domains, which becomes increasingly useful as the world gets too complex for any one person to understand everything directly.

### What happens when you don't know?

Sometimes I may ask about something the person probably hasn't encountered before. I am not necessarily expecting the magical correct answer.

I want to see what happens next.

Can you reason from what you already know? Do you notice where an analogy works and where it starts breaking down? Can you work out what information you would need before making the decision?

Someone might say:

> I haven't dealt with that exact situation before, but it reminds me of...

and then reason from there.

I like that much more than watching somebody desperately try to convince me that they somehow know everything.

## Communication makes all of that visible

You can know your stuff, have delivered genuinely difficult work and be very good at connecting ideas, but the panel still needs to discover that during a fairly short conversation.

This is where communication matters.

Good communicators can be seen from a mile away. I don't mean someone with an impressive accent or a vocabulary full of big words; I mean somebody who can take a complicated idea, understand what matters to the people listening and explain it without making everyone excavate the useful point from five minutes of background.

Confidence matters too, although I try not to confuse confidence with competence. There are people who can confidently explain complete nonsense for a very long time.

The confidence I like comes from knowing where you stand.

> I don't know, but this is how I would work it out.

said calmly can be much stronger than an elaborate answer built on bullshit.

The interview preparation I do cannot create the substance. It can help make sure the substance actually comes through.

## Passion leaks out eventually

I also look for whether someone genuinely cares about what they are talking about.

You can memorise enough about almost any subject to get through the first few interview questions. You can learn all the fashionable words too, and after enough Googling probably produce a pretty convincing answer about why the subject is important.

Genuine interest gets harder to fake once you keep digging.

People who really care often know some weird little detail nobody required them to know. They have gone down some rabbit hole because a question bothered them, tried something nobody asked them to try, or kept reading long after they had enough information to finish the immediate piece of work.

I recognise this because I do exactly the same thing when something catches my interest.

That does not mean somebody has to bounce around the room shouting about how passionate they are. Some people are naturally quiet, and loud enthusiasm can obviously be performed too.

I am more interested in the traces that curiosity leaves behind.

## Character and intent matter too

This is much harder to assess, and I don't pretend that a 45-minute interview gives me magical powers to see whether somebody has a good heart.

Still, genuine intent and character matter to me, particularly for leadership roles in the public service. Public service core competency, anyone?

You sometimes get glimpses from the way somebody tells a story. When something succeeded, did everybody else disappear from it? When something failed, was somebody else always somehow responsible?

I also listen to how they talk about junior staff, difficult colleagues and people who disagreed with them. If they had to make a difficult decision, what seemed to matter when they made it?

This is obviously imperfect. People can perform virtue in interviews as easily as they can perform passion.

But competence without good intent can do quite a lot of damage once somebody has enough authority, so I would rather probe it than pretend character doesn't matter because it is difficult to measure.

## So what am I actually looking for?

If I had to summarise the things I personally want to understand from an interview, it would be something like this:

- **Do you know your stuff?** There should be real depth behind the work you say you have done.
- **Can you get stuff done?** I want to distinguish ownership and execution from merely being near successful work.
- **What can you do with what you know?** I care about judgement, connecting dots and what happens when the problem is unfamiliar.
- **Can you communicate it?** The rest of us need to understand what is happening inside your head.
- **Are you genuinely interested?** Curiosity usually leaves evidence in the details and rabbit holes nobody asked you to explore.
- **What kind of person are you when things get difficult?** Intent, judgement and how you treat other people matter, especially once you become a leader.

This is also why I don't think interview preparation should mainly be about memorising the perfect answers.

You cannot prepare most of those things the night before.

What you _can_ do is make sure the panel gets a decent chance to see them.

# How I prepare before the interview

I don't interview often, unfortunately. What I do practise quite a lot is the specific interview in the days before it happens.

This is where I become slightly obsessive.

## Thinking about an answer isn't practising it

The biggest thing I do is say my answers out loud.

It is very easy to sit somewhere thinking:

> Yes yes, I know how I will answer that.

Then somebody actually asks you the question and three minutes later you are still explaining the background while the brilliant answer that existed inside your head has somehow disappeared.

This happens to me too. An answer that sounds concise in my head can become ridiculously long once I hear myself saying it, and sometimes a beautiful sentence I wrote down turns out to be something no normal version of me would ever actually say.

So I talk to myself.

I might stand in front of a mirror, walk around the room saying an answer repeatedly, or read it aloud until I find a version that sounds natural.

### I don't memorise paragraphs

I sometimes write an answer out initially because writing helps me organise my thoughts. What I don't want is to memorise the whole thing and reproduce it word for word.

The interviewer will inevitably ask something slightly different and suddenly my beautiful prepared answer does not fit.

What I really want in my head are the main points, some useful examples and perhaps one or two sentences that express something particularly well.

A difficult project I worked on could potentially help answer questions about leadership, disagreement, recovering from a mistake or making trade-offs. I should be able to use the experience differently depending on what they actually ask.

## I record myself

I also try to record myself answering questions.

This is mildly horrible.

Watching yourself talk is a very effective way of discovering all the weird things you apparently do without knowing. Maybe I keep looking away whenever I think, move around too much, or discover that what felt like a confident answer somehow looks hesitant on video.

I am not trying to manufacture some executive body language or choreograph where my hands should be every five seconds. I mostly want to remove distracting habits and become a little more aware of how I come across.

Recording also exposes how long my answers actually are.

If I thought something was a quick response and discover I have been talking for four minutes... okay, maybe it wasn't that quick.

## I rehearse the first few lines

I pay particular attention to the beginning of the interview because that is usually when I am most conscious of being interviewed.

Once the conversation gets going, I generally settle down.

So I rehearse the first few lines. I think about how I will introduce myself, what I might say if they start with some version of "tell us about yourself", and how I want the first answer to begin.

I don't need the next 45 minutes scripted.

I just want a comfortable runway into the interview so that I am not dealing with the initial nerves while also inventing my opening from scratch.

## I visualise the entire thing

I also prepare in a way that reminds me of sports competition.

I picture the whole event beforehand, including the boring parts. I imagine arriving, walking into the room, greeting everyone, sitting down and hearing the first question.

Then I picture the interview getting underway. Somebody asks something difficult. I pause and think. Eventually we reach the end and I walk out again.

If the interview is online, I do basically the same thing with joining the call and seeing everybody appear on screen.

I am not imagining some fantasy where every answer is perfect and the panel spontaneously applauds.

The point is to make the whole experience feel familiar enough that, when it actually happens, I have fewer new things to process.

Athletes visualise their competition. I suppose I am doing a much nerdier office-worker version of the same thing.

## Prepare for the predictable questions

There will always be questions you didn't expect, but come on... interviews aren't completely random events.

You can normally anticipate quite a bit from the role, your own experience and whatever the organisation is dealing with.

For a leadership interview, I might prepare around questions like:

- Why do I want this role?
- What experience do I have that is particularly relevant?
- What difficult situation have I dealt with, and what did I actually do?
- What have I got wrong before?
- How do I deal with disagreement, especially with somebody senior?
- What does my leadership style look like in practice?
- What is likely to be difficult about this role?
- What would I do differently if I encountered a similar situation again?

I don't need perfect answers to every one of these.

I just don't want the interview to be the first time I have seriously thought about them.

## Especially prepare the questions you hope they don't ask

The uncomfortable questions are probably even more useful.

If there is a weakness in my experience, something that went badly or some awkward part of a project somebody could reasonably challenge, I would rather think about it beforehand than sit there silently hoping nobody notices.

The same applies to presentations. I look at the deck and ask which recommendation is likely to annoy someone, where my evidence is weakest and whether there is a slide where I am quietly praying nobody asks the obvious follow-up.

If there is, that is probably the question I should practise.

There is something quite comforting about getting a difficult question in the actual session and realising I have already wrestled with some version of it before.

I may still need to think.

At least I am not starting from zero.

## Know what you want them to remember

The panel controls the questions, but I still go into an interview knowing what I hope they learn about me.

There are usually a few experiences or things about how I work that are particularly relevant, and I don't want to walk out afterwards thinking:

> Shit, we never talked about that at all.

This doesn't mean forcing the same favourite story into every unrelated question.

It means knowing what material I have and recognising an opening when one appears.

I do something similar with presentations. If the audience remembers only a few things the next morning, I should probably know beforehand what I want those things to be.

# I wish I went for more interviews

I remember hearing ThePrimeagen give advice along the lines of interviewing at places you don't particularly want to go to, so that you get some real practice before interviewing somewhere you really care about.

I thought that was good advice.

Unfortunately, I didn't really follow it... and I wish I had.

Because I don't interview often, the important interview can also become the occasion when I am shaking off the rust. Mirror practice, recording myself and visualising the session all help, but none of them fully reproduce sitting opposite people who are genuinely deciding whether they want you.

Real interviews give you awkward pauses and questions you misunderstood. You meet the panel member whose face reveals absolutely nothing, and sometimes you get to experience an answer going somewhere completely unexpected and having to recover from it.

Those reps probably matter.

If you are genuinely open to a few roles, even if some are not the one you desperately want, I can see the value in doing the interviews rather than saving your first one in five years for the job you really care about.

I am writing this partly as advice to myself...

# Prepare your final question

There is one interview question you can almost certainly prepare for:

> Do you have any questions for us?

I think people underestimate this part.

I normally want at least one question ready, and I am quite deliberate about the type of question I prepare.

Ideally, it should:

- be something I genuinely want answered;
- not have an answer sitting on the organisation's website;
- get the panel talking about something important to the role;
- give them something they can answer partly, with enough room for the conversation to come back to me;
- give me something useful to add if they eventually ask what I think.

For example:

> What do you think will be the hardest problem for the person coming into this role?

They might tell me the team is struggling with a particular issue, that expectations are changing, or that whoever comes in will have to deal with some difficult stakeholders.

I listen because I genuinely want to know.

But there is also a reasonable chance that somebody eventually turns it around:

> What do you think?

or:

> How would you approach that?

Great!

I have just been given another opportunity to show how I think, except this time I knew roughly what I wanted to say before I entered the room.

Yes, I absolutely think about this beforehand.

It is still an interview. If I can ask a genuine question, learn something useful about the role and create another chance to demonstrate that I understand the problem, why wouldn't I?

## Don't force it though

Sometimes they answer the question completely and that is the end of the interview.

Fine.

Please don't respond:

> Oh actually, can I tell you what I think about that?

If they give you half an answer and there is a natural opening, engage with it. Maybe you mention briefly that you have dealt with something similar, then see whether they want to ask you more.

The idea is to create a platform for another useful exchange.

It isn't a licence to bolt another five-minute sales pitch onto the end after everybody is obviously trying to leave.

# Am I still myself in interviews?

Someone asked whether I remain my "true authentic self" during interviews or put on a different image.

I think I am still myself, obviously a more deliberate and probably better-behaved version.

An interview isn't lunch with my friends. I know I am being assessed, so of course I choose my words more carefully and suppress some of the stupid shit I might otherwise say.

I don't suddenly become incredibly agreeable or pretend to believe in some management philosophy because I think that is what the panel wants to hear.

If I have a strong view about something relevant, I would rather say what I actually think and explain why.

After all, if I get the job, somebody eventually has to employ the real me.

And I have to work there too.

If they only like me because I successfully hid everything inconvenient about how I think or work during the interview, that could become a rather annoying discovery for everybody three months later.

## Preparation helps me be more myself

This is also why I don't see rehearsal as being fake.

I am practising how to express what I genuinely think better.

The first version of an honest answer can still be rubbish. Maybe I ramble, give too much context or take four minutes to reach the useful bit.

Practising lets me clean that up without changing the underlying view.

Once I know my material, I also have more attention available for the people in front of me. I can listen properly, shorten an answer when they clearly get it, or go deeper when somebody seems interested.

So yes, I manage how I present myself.

Everybody does.

I just don't want to manufacture the person underneath.

# Big presentations are very similar

I prepare for important presentations in much the same way.

The slides themselves usually worry me less because I control them. I know what comes next and what I intend to say, while the discussion afterwards is where other people get to take the conversation somewhere I didn't plan.

So I practise the presentation aloud and think quite a lot about what happens after the final slide.

Who is likely to disagree?

Where is the argument weakest?

What question do I really hope nobody asks?

Sometimes I practise how I will introduce the difficult bits too.

Imagine a room full of people who have spent years supporting an existing approach, and my slide effectively says:

> We recommend stopping the existing approach because the evidence no longer supports it.

That sentence looks perfectly reasonable sitting here in a blog post.

Actually saying it in the room is different. There is history behind the decision, people may have invested quite a bit of themselves in it, and everybody is now looking at me.

I don't want the first time I hear myself say the difficult sentence to be when all the important people are staring back.

# The interview is mostly revealing what is already there

I suppose this is where I eventually land.

Interview preparation matters. I rehearse aloud, use a mirror, record myself, visualise the session, prepare likely questions and even think about the question I am going to ask at the end.

All of that helps.

But none of it substitutes for knowing your stuff.

It cannot manufacture years of getting things done, and it cannot suddenly give you the ability to connect ideas from different domains because you memorised a framework the night before.

Most of the work happened before the interview was even scheduled.

The final preparation is there so that, when somebody gives you an hour to show what you know, what you have done and what you might be capable of next, you don't accidentally hide all of it behind nerves and a five-minute ramble.
